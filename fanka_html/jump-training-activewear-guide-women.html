What to Wear for Jump Training: A Women’s Activewear Guide

What to Wear for Jump Training: A Women’s Activewear Guide

By Fanka Editor | Oct 15th, 2026 | 7 Min Read

Jump training can mean a low pogo hop, a line jump, a small squat jump, or a more demanding plyometric drill. Whatever the level, your activewear should stay quiet while your body leaves the floor and lands again: no slipping waist, swinging phone, rolling hem, or straps that need constant adjustment.

This guide is educational, not medical advice. Fanka products are not medical-grade. If you have pain, osteoporosis, pelvic-floor symptoms, balance concerns, are pregnant or postpartum, or have been advised to limit impact, discuss jump training with a qualified healthcare professional before starting.

Understand What Your Jump Session Includes

The word “plyometrics” covers a wide range. A beginner session may use small, controlled hops with generous rest. A sport session may add lateral bounds, repeated jumps, or faster direction changes. Dress for the actual movement and duration—not for the most dramatic version you saw online.

Choose Support That Stays Put

A sports bra should feel secure through the whole jump—not only while standing in front of a mirror. The band should sit level, the cups or front panel should contain you without spilling, and straps should not dig in or slide. Support needs vary by body and session, so use movement to decide.

Try a three-move bra check

  • Reach both arms overhead and confirm the band does not ride up.
  • Jog gently in place, then make two low hops.
  • Take a full breath; support should not feel like restricted breathing.

A fitted tank can reduce fabric movement, while a light removable layer is useful between sets. Avoid tying a heavy sweatshirt around your waist during jumps because it can shift your balance or hide your landing position.

Check Leggings Before You Add Impact

For jump training, leggings should stay at the waist, remain smooth behind the knees, and finish at a length that does not catch your heel. Compression can feel supportive, but tighter is not automatically better. You should be able to breathe, squat, and land without numbness, pinching, or restricted movement.

The five-move Fanka check: squat, hinge, lateral step, calf raise, and two low hops. If the waistband rolls, the crotch drops, or seams rub during this short check, adjust the size or choose another pair before starting a full session.

Keep keys and phones out of loose pockets. A zipped or close-fitting pocket can work for easy drills, but the safest choice for a focused indoor session is often to place your belongings beside the training area.

Build Your First Jump-Training Outfit

Start with a simple base: a supportive bra or bra tank, full-length or 7/8 leggings that clear the heel, socks that do not bunch, and shoes chosen for your floor and coach’s guidance. Add only the layers you need for the warm-up and cool-down.

FAQ

Do I need compression leggings for jump training?
No. Choose leggings or shorts that stay in place and allow full movement. Some women prefer a supportive compression feel, while others move better in a lighter fabric.
Can activewear protect my knees or bones?
No garment can make an impact session medically safe or prevent injury. Fanka products are not medical-grade. Ask a qualified professional about pain, bone health, balance, or impact restrictions.
What if my leggings slide down when I land?
Stop and adjust rather than pulling them up through the whole workout. Recheck the rise, waistband, size, pocket load, and whether the fabric is suited to repeated movement.
What is the easiest way to start?
Use a coached beginner progression with low, controlled hops and plenty of rest. Movement-test your outfit first, clear the training area, and stop if you feel pain or concerning symptoms.

Final Thoughts

The best jump-training outfit is the one you stop noticing. Start with secure support, a stable waistband, clear hems, and empty pockets. Then let a sensible progression—not your clothes or a social-media challenge—set the pace.
